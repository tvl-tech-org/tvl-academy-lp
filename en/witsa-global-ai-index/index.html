01 — The appointment

A seat on the panel, representing Romania

WITSA, the World Innovation, Technology and Services Alliance, is a global alliance of technology industry associations. For the inaugural Global AI Index it convened an independent expert panel of 22 members drawn from 18 economies. Alexandru Dan represented Romania on that panel, listed as founder and CEO of TVL Tech, alongside experts from bodies such as the OECD, the World Bank, Stanford University, and Harvard Kennedy School.

The panel was not decorative. Under the index Terms of Reference, it held authority over the full indicator list, the sub-pillar framework, the pillar weights, the sign-off on the methodology document, and the conflict-of-interest rules. The report's own chapter about the panel is built around a single claim: the experts had the authority to change the methodology, and they used it.

02 — What the index measures

Ecosystems that were built, not announced

The index scores AI ecosystem development, the structural conditions that decide whether AI produces durable national value, not the announcements or the strategy documents behind it. It covers 128 economies across seven pillars: Infrastructure, Talent, Entrepreneurship, Use Cases, Data, Policy, and Investments. Underneath sit 16 sub-pillars and 83 indicators, built to the OECD/JRC Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators.

Scores are per-capita by design. The question is how deeply an economy has built AI capability relative to its own population, not how much it has amassed in total, so a small country building intensely is not buried by a large one. Each pillar runs from 0 to 100 against the observed global frontier for that measure, so 100 means matching the strongest measured performer this cycle, not perfection.

  • Infrastructure — compute, connectivity, and the physical layer AI runs on.
  • Talent — researchers, developers, and the people who build with AI.
  • Entrepreneurship — startups, unicorns, and the pace of company formation.
  • Use Cases — real adoption of AI inside organizations.
  • Data — open data, datasets, and published models.
  • Policy — governance, strategy, and AI readiness of the state.
  • Investments — funding, greenfield capital, and financial commitment.
03 — Why it is credible

Open sources, and open to challenge

WITSA carries no vendor interest and no government affiliation, which is the case for letting it run the index. Every indicator has a documented source. Every imputed value is flagged. Every economy can contest a specific indicator value through a formal data review process run by the Secretariat.

The methodology is published in full and was reviewed by 22 independent experts before they would validate it. Economies are sorted into Group A, B, or C purely by how much comparable data exists, never by any judgment about how advanced they are. Anyone can trace a score back to its indicators, and anyone with better data can challenge it.

04 — Romania's scorecard

Strong on data and skills, thin on capital

Romania is a fully scored Group A economy with an overall Ecosystem Development Score of 48.0. The number splits unevenly across the seven pillars.

Source: WITSA Global AI Index 2026, confirmed pillar scores (final frozen run). Pillars run 0 to 100 on a per-capita basis.

Romania's clear strength is Data, at 87.7, one of the higher data scores in Europe. Talent follows at 59.9 and Policy at 55.4, both above the overall. The constraints sit on the commercial and physical side: Entrepreneurship at 25.6, Investments at 31.4, and Infrastructure at 33.9, with Use Cases in the middle at 42.3.

This is an economy with strong human capital and a strong data foundation whose AI ecosystem is held back by weak capital formation and a thin physical layer. The skills and the data are in place; the money and the machines are not yet. For scale, the index is led by the United States at 82.2, Singapore at 82.0, and Sweden at 79.9. Luxembourg, where Alexandru chairs the AI Committee of the Romania Luxembourg Business Forum, scores 59.2 overall, led by Talent at 83.2.

05 — Why it matters for companies

A national score is made of company decisions

Romania's weak spots, turning capability and capital into deployed use cases, are exactly what shows up inside individual firms as AI that stalls after the pilot. Teams experiment, get inconsistent results, and quietly give up. The national Use Cases pillar is the sum of that.

Closing that gap is the work: getting teams past experiments and into systems they use every week. The TVL Academy program does exactly this, with prompt libraries, per-role AI assistants, and an automation plan built on a company's real processes. It targets the Use Cases pillar directly, the one where Romania scores lowest and the one a single company can actually change.
